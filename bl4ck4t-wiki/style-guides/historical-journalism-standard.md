# Historical Journalism Standard

## Summary

Historical cybersecurity accounts for BL4CK4T seasons must be researched and written with journalistic discipline before any fictional transformation begins. The goal is to make the truth more fascinating than the myth.

## Core Principle

Do not make cybersecurity sound magical. Make the real causal chain, human drama, technical stakes, uncertainty, and consequences clear enough that the later BL4CK4T story rests on fact rather than mythology.

## Required Rules

### Separate Claim Types

Every major claim should be classifiable as:

- Known fact: supported by primary or strong near-primary evidence.
- Allegation: claimed by an actor such as a government, company, law-enforcement body, researcher, journalist, or participant but not independently proven.
- Reasonable inference: drawn from available evidence such as timing, code similarity, infrastructure, victimology, operational pattern, or context.
- Unknown: unresolved or unavailable from public evidence.
- Disputed: contested by credible sources or interpretations.

Never flatten these categories for narrative force.

### Preserve Moral Complexity

Do not turn hackers into superheroes or cartoon villains. Preserve the possibility that people can be brilliant and reckless, curious and harmful, exploited and responsible, patriotic and dangerous, or diligent and ignored.

### Explain Mechanisms Plainly

Explain the actual mechanism without exaggeration or magical language. The reader should understand the causal chain at the level needed to understand the event, while avoiding unnecessary operational reproduction.

### Preserve Timeline And Knowledge State

Move through time honestly:

- Something strange happened.
- Someone noticed symptoms.
- Theories formed.
- Evidence accumulated.
- Public understanding changed.
- Later analysis revised the story.

Do not write early scenes as if participants already knew later conclusions.

### Prefer Primary Sources

Use primary or near-primary sources whenever possible:

- Court records.
- Indictments.
- CERT advisories.
- Malware analyses.
- Contemporaneous mailing-list posts.
- Incident reports.
- Technical writeups by original investigators.
- Archived reporting from the time.
- Interviews with participants.
- Source code where available.
- Conference talks by people directly involved.
- Government reports.
- Company postmortems.

Secondary summaries are useful for orientation, not as the sole basis for central claims.

### Treat Attribution As A Discipline

Attribution must explain evidence and confidence. Consider technical indicators, infrastructure, malware lineage, operational timing, victim selection, language artifacts, strategic motive, official claims, independent research, and alternative explanations. Avoid statements like "X did Y" unless the evidence standard is explained.

### Avoid Monocausal Explanations

Major events usually involve interacting systems: defaults, incentives, technical assumptions, user behavior, legal gaps, institutional blind spots, fragile infrastructure, and response maturity. Show the system that allowed the event to matter.

### Show Attacker And Defender Stories

The defender story is not secondary. Identify who noticed first, what clue seemed too small, who was ignored, who connected the dots, what defenders lacked, what they built under pressure, and what they misunderstood.

### Measure Significance Carefully

Do not confuse scale with significance. Evaluate technical novelty, historical consequence, legal impact, cultural impact, geopolitical impact, economic damage, defensive-practice change, and public imagination.

### Be Careful With Damage Estimates

Damage estimates must be sourced and caveated. Explain whether estimates include downtime, lost revenue, recovery costs, legal fees, insurance claims, hardware replacement, reputational damage, government response, or future security investment.

### Avoid Presentism

Explain the norms, laws, tools, assumptions, and maturity level of the time. Do not judge past actors as if they had today's security culture, while still preserving accountability for harm.

### Resist Loose "First Ever" Claims

Avoid dramatic firsts unless narrowly true. Prefer phrases like "one of the first major," "a landmark case," "among the earliest publicly known," or "the first widely recognized instance under this definition."

### Include Legal And Policy Aftermath

Explain what changed in law, policy, doctrine, defensive practice, industry behavior, or public imagination after the event.

### Give Victims Agency And Specificity

Victims are not scenery. Describe affected people, institutions, defenders, operators, administrators, citizens, companies, governments, and responders with specificity.

### Explain The Infrastructure

The attacked infrastructure is a character in the history. Explain the telephone network, early Internet, industrial control systems, national digital services, enterprise update pipelines, or open-source dependency chains before the narrative depends on them.

### Preserve Uncertainty

Uncertainty can be part of the drama. State what the public record shows, what investigators concluded, what officials claimed, and what remains unanswered.

### Beware Attacker-Supplied Mythology

Ask who benefits from a given version of the story. Hackers, intelligence services, criminal groups, governments, companies, and researchers may all have incentives to exaggerate, hide, reframe, or mythologize.

### Avoid Operationally Harmful Detail

Explain mechanisms, consequences, and defensive lessons. Avoid unnecessary exploit chains, credentials, live infrastructure, bypass methods, replication steps, or practical abuse instructions.

### Interview Across Roles When Possible

For deeper accounts, seek perspectives from investigators, administrators, reverse engineers, responders, victims, lawyers, policy officials, maintainers, journalists, academics, law enforcement, and people who disagree with dominant narratives.

### Follow Incentives

Ask who had incentives to reveal, hide, exaggerate, minimize, monetize, politicize, or reframe the event.

### Treat Cybersecurity As Human History

Include curiosity, pride, fear, carelessness, patriotism, greed, burnout, obsession, bureaucratic blindness, loneliness, moral compromise, and quiet diligence where the sources support it.

### Make Technical Stakes Material

Use accurate visual or material metaphors for later story transformation: worms as self-copying instructions, DDoS as overwhelmed roads, supply-chain compromise as poisoned updates, and XZ-style near misses as tiny hidden gears. Metaphor should clarify, not distort.

### Preserve Contingency

Avoid making events look inevitable. Show where events could have unfolded differently and where small observations, missed chances, or delayed decisions mattered.

### Include Dissenting Interpretations

Present strong responsible versions of competing interpretations for contested events. Do not flatten Estonia, Mitnick, Stuxnet, NotPetya, XZ Utils, or similar stories into one unchallenged narrative when credible debate exists.

### End With What Changed

Every historical account should answer what permanently changed: doctrine, law, response practice, technical architecture, ecosystem trust, public imagination, or policy.

## Practical Checklist

Before a historical account is marked complete, it must answer:

1. What are the primary sources for the central claims?
2. Which claims are known, alleged, inferred, disputed, or unknown?
3. What did people know at the time, and what was discovered later?
4. What technical mechanism actually caused the event?
5. What is the strongest alternative interpretation?
6. Who benefits from the dominant version of the story?
7. What is simplified for narrative reasons?
8. Are damage estimates sourced and explained?
9. Are victims treated as real people and institutions?
10. Does the story explain what changed afterward?
