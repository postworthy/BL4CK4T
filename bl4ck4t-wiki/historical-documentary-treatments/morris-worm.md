# Morris Worm Documentary Treatment

## Purpose

This treatment turns the sourced Morris Worm account into a nonfiction movement structure before any fictional adaptation. It sharpens suspense, causality, and consequence while keeping the history separate from story-world invention.

## Source Boundary

Primary account: [Morris Worm](../historical-accounts/morris-worm.md).

Do not add fictional names, fictional places, or project-world transformation in this document. Do not provide operational worm-building instructions.

## Documentary Thesis

The Morris Worm was a turning point because a non-destructive experiment in a trust-heavy network still caused real disruption, forced improvised response, and changed how connected systems were governed.

## Cold Open

A terminal slows. A login hangs. A message about an attack moves through a network that is itself becoming harder to use. The opening question: how can a program that does not erase files still bring work to a halt?

## Movement Structure

### Movement 1: The Young Network

- Historical function: establish the late-1980s Internet as a small, research-heavy, trust-heavy network.
- Narrative question: what assumptions held the network together?
- Key beats: Unix systems, universities, research labs, government links, pre-web culture.
- Evidence anchors: FBI retrospective; Cornell Commission; court opinion.
- Visual/audio language: terminals, login prompts, lab rooms, network maps, late-night offices.
- Uncertainty guardrails: avoid implying the commercial web existed.

### Movement 2: The Experiment

- Historical function: introduce Morris's skill, access, and stated security-interest context.
- Narrative question: what did the program appear designed to do?
- Key beats: October work, release through MIT, desire for wide hidden spread.
- Evidence anchors: court opinion; Cornell Commission.
- Visual/audio language: code as sealed pages, a launch point, a hidden origin.
- Uncertainty guardrails: distinguish known design from subjective motive.

### Movement 3: The Copying Problem

- Historical function: explain reinfection as the central failure without step-by-step mechanics.
- Narrative question: why did the spread exceed expectation?
- Key beats: check for existing copy, anti-suppression choice, repeated copying, resource exhaustion.
- Evidence anchors: court opinion; Spafford technical report; Cornell Commission.
- Visual/audio language: counters climbing, memory filling, duplicated process cards.
- Uncertainty guardrails: explain causality without providing a recipe.

### Movement 4: The Network As Victim

- Historical function: show impact through responders and institutions.
- Narrative question: what does harm look like when files survive but work stops?
- Key beats: slowed systems, disconnections, wiped systems, delayed email, interrupted research.
- Evidence anchors: FBI; Cornell Commission; NSA postmortem.
- Visual/audio language: phones ringing, printers stopped, isolated terminals.
- Uncertainty guardrails: use cost and infection estimates as ranges.

### Movement 5: Improvised Response

- Historical function: show defenders coordinating before mature incident-response structures existed.
- Narrative question: who do you call when the network itself is the emergency?
- Key beats: site reports, postmortem meeting, technical analysis, delayed anonymous guidance.
- Evidence anchors: NSA postmortem; Spafford; FBI; court opinion.
- Visual/audio language: bulletin notes, cross-site calls, handwritten fixes, network congestion.
- Uncertainty guardrails: do not make response look instant or centralized.

### Movement 6: Court And Consequence

- Historical function: connect technical harm to legal accountability.
- Narrative question: what did the law decide mattered?
- Key beats: FBI investigation, indictment, conviction, appeal, sentence.
- Evidence anchors: United States v. Morris; FBI.
- Visual/audio language: court documents, statutory text, probation order, community service hours.
- Uncertainty guardrails: do not claim intent to destroy files.

### Movement 7: What Changed

- Historical function: show lasting impact.
- Narrative question: how did one incident change security practice?
- Key beats: CERT/CC, intrusion detection, incident coordination, safe testing lessons.
- Evidence anchors: FBI; CMU SEI.
- Visual/audio language: response center, contact lists, advisories, training rooms.
- Uncertainty guardrails: avoid claiming the incident alone created all modern cybersecurity.

## Recurring Motifs

- Copy count: moves from harmless-looking repetition to system pressure.
- Delay: guidance, email, and decisions arrive late because the network is impaired.
- Boundaries: lab, network, legal, and ethical boundaries blur and then harden.
- Work stoppage: harm appears as stalled research, unavailable machines, and response labor.

## Interview Targets Or Archival Voices

- Court opinion: legal voice and timeline.
- Cornell Commission: university investigation and intent analysis.
- Spafford report: responder technical voice.
- NSA postmortem: institutional response and site experiences.
- FBI retrospective: investigation and public legacy.
- CMU SEI history: emergency response aftermath.

## Narration Guardrails

- Claims to state plainly: release date, authorship, broad spread, no file destruction, resource exhaustion, conviction, CERT/CC aftermath.
- Claims to qualify: exact number infected, total cost, Morris's subjective motive.
- Claims to avoid: cyberwar framing, "first ever worm" without qualification, operational replication detail.

## Ending

End with the shift from a trust-heavy network to a security culture that understands containment, coordination, and accountability as part of building connected systems.
