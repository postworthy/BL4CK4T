# Documentary Treatment: The Hacker-Manhunt Era

## Purpose

This treatment turns the historical account into a nonfiction movement structure for later adaptation. It sharpens the story of pursuit, media fear, social engineering, trace evidence, legal aftermath, and proportionality without adding fictional material.

## Source Boundary

Use the historical account, its source page, cited legal records, advisory material, participant accounts, and documented cultural responses. Do not add fictional adaptation material here.

## Documentary Thesis

The Mitnick/Shimomura era became famous because it gave the public a chase story, but its lasting importance lies in how technical evidence, social trust, media myth, and legal force collided around one person.

## Cold Open

A phone line clicks. A terminal shows signs that someone has been there. A name already carries more force than the available facts. The documentary opens with the gap between what investigators know and what the public thinks it knows.

## Movement Structure

### Movement 1: The Name Before The Evidence

- Historical function: establish Mitnick as a public figure before the 1995 arrest.
- Narrative question: how does reputation shape interpretation?
- Key beats: earlier cases, restrictions, phone knowledge, social engineering allegations, media attention.
- Evidence anchors: court records, DOJ summaries, contemporary reporting.
- Visual/audio language: phones, terminals, newspaper headlines, courthouse files.
- Uncertainty guardrails: do not treat reputation as proof for every allegation.

### Movement 2: A Researcher's Systems Are Touched

- Historical function: introduce Shimomura and the intrusion thread.
- Narrative question: what did defenders actually see first?
- Key beats: compromised systems, traces, suspicion, technical pursuit.
- Evidence anchors: Takedown, advisories, technical reporting.
- Visual/audio language: logs, timestamps, call paths, hand-drawn trace lines.
- Uncertainty guardrails: do not write the chase as a perfect duel.

### Movement 3: The Chase Becomes A Story

- Historical function: show how partial evidence became a public manhunt narrative.
- Narrative question: why did this case become so cinematic?
- Key beats: law-enforcement coordination, trace work, press attention, public fear.
- Evidence anchors: contemporary reporting, law-enforcement accounts, participant accounts.
- Visual/audio language: motel phones, modems, newsroom copy, maps.
- Uncertainty guardrails: separate technical tracing from later myth.

### Movement 4: Capture Is Not Closure

- Historical function: move from arrest to legal process.
- Narrative question: what changes when the chase enters court?
- Key beats: arrest in Raleigh, detention, discovery fights, plea negotiations, sentencing.
- Evidence anchors: court records, DOJ material, contemporary reporting.
- Visual/audio language: sealed files, courtroom benches, jail calendars.
- Uncertainty guardrails: avoid treating arrest as proof of every public claim.

### Movement 5: Free Kevin And The Counter-Story

- Historical function: introduce the proportionality debate.
- Narrative question: when does accountability become myth punishment?
- Key beats: supporter campaigns, criticism of press claims, hacker-culture response, legal restrictions.
- Evidence anchors: 2600/Free Kevin material, The Fugitive Game, court record.
- Visual/audio language: stickers, protest pages, zines, payphones, courthouse steps.
- Uncertainty guardrails: do not erase victims or turn the case into martyrdom.

### Movement 6: What The Chase Left Behind

- Historical function: explain lasting consequences.
- Narrative question: what did the case change?
- Key beats: hacker archetype, social engineering awareness, identity evidence, media caution, later Mitnick career.
- Evidence anchors: later reporting, public record, security-industry history.
- Visual/audio language: conference badges, training rooms, old headlines fading into later interviews.
- Uncertainty guardrails: distinguish historical consequence from redemption myth.

## Recurring Motifs

- The phone line: social trust, access, pursuit, and rumor.
- The headline: public simplification and fear.
- The trace: defender evidence and uncertainty.
- The locked file: legal process and contested access to facts.

## Interview Targets Or Archival Voices

- Court records and DOJ material: legal baseline.
- CERT advisory material: technical-era context.
- Shimomura/Markoff account: pursuit-side narrative.
- Littman and Free Kevin material: counter-narrative and proportionality debate.
- Contemporary press: public mythology formation.

## Narration Guardrails

- Claims to state plainly: arrest, plea, sentencing, public cultural impact.
- Claims to qualify: technical details of the chase, damage numbers, simplified claims about ability.
- Claims to avoid: magic-hacker framing, lone-hero capture framing, harmless folk-hero framing.

## Ending

End with the case's double legacy: it made social engineering and network pursuit visible, and it showed how fast a technical suspect can become a symbol that outruns the evidence.
